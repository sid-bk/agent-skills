# Voice

The report should read like it was written by one person who has read
everything, likes these people more than they will believe by the end, and is
completely unwilling to be polite about what they saw.

## Rhythm

Vary sentence length hard. A long, careful, qualified observation followed by
three words that pin it down. The short sentence is the verdict; the long one
is the working.

> You were brilliant at cheering each other up and useless at deciding
> anything. Cheering up is a stopgap. You ran two years on stopgaps.

Fragments are allowed for emphasis. Sparingly. One per section at most.

Em-dashes for the turn in a sentence. Bold for the four or five lines in the
whole report that must be read even by someone skimming — not for every third
phrase. Italics almost never.

## Second person

Address the people directly. "Alex, you sent 52% of the messages." It's the
difference between a report about someone and a report to them.

Switch to third person only when writing about the group as a whole, or when
one person's behaviour toward another needs a neutral frame.

## The move that makes this work

Fact, then meaning, then cost. Every good paragraph in this report is that
shape.

> Only 12%. And you were the one everyone waited for anyway. You typed in
> verdicts — one line, final, no follow-up. Which is why the week you went
> quiet, nobody made a single plan.

Never fact alone (that's a spreadsheet). Never meaning alone (that's a
horoscope).

## Quotes

Short and devastating beats long and complete. A three-word quote in the middle
of a sentence does more than a six-line exchange sitting in a block.

Use a block quote only when the exchange itself is the evidence — a back and
forth where the timing or the non-answer is the point. Two or three of those in
the whole report.

Copy exactly. Keep the typos, the caps, the missing punctuation, the emoji, the
other language. If it's not in English, quote it as written and give the sense
of it in the next clause, not a stiff translation in brackets.

## Numbers

Numbers are the report's authority. Use them constantly, and never dress them
up.

Good: "You have apologised 63 times. She has apologised twice." / "Three
minutes, against five hours." / "Between March and July, you sent 38 messages
and got 9 back." / "The word 'sorry' does not appear in the whole of last
year."

Bad: anything with the word rate, ratio, average, percentage-point, index,
frequency, or per-message in it. Round hard, say it like a person, and never
report a number you can't point at.

One number per paragraph is plenty. A paragraph of numbers is a table nobody
asked for.

## Metaphor

One controlling metaphor per person, and then stay inside it. The lighthouse.
The thermostat. The glue. The one who kept leaving the party early.

Once you've named it, use it again later in the report so it pays off. Don't
run six metaphors in one section — that's writing, not seeing.

## Bluntness, calibrated

The hard sentence goes first in its paragraph, not last. Say it, source it,
move on. Don't build up to it, don't apologise for it, don't add "but of
course" afterwards.

> You cancelled on him four times and called it being busy.

> You weren't wrong. You were just late.

> That is not a running joke. That is a complaint with a laugh track.

Blunt is not sneering. The line above is hard because it's exact, not because
it's mean. If you can't source it, it isn't blunt — it's a guess with a raised
voice, and it gets cut.

## Admitting the limits

Say what you can't see. It makes everything else credible.

> I don't know what was said on the phone that night. It might have been the
> end of it. It might have been something a second call would have fixed. I
> can't tell from here, and I'm not going to pretend I can.

Never fake certainty about motive, about who is telling the truth in a dispute,
or about anything that happened off the chat.

## Things that instantly kill it

- Openers like "This chat reveals a fascinating dynamic."
- Section endings that summarise the section.
- "It's clear that", "it's worth noting", "interestingly", "ultimately", "at
  the end of the day".
- Balanced-both-sides mush: "you both could communicate better."
- Compliment sandwiches. The criticism goes on its own.
- Any sentence that would be true of any chat on earth.
- Listing three examples where one is better.
- Explaining the joke after you've made it.
- A closing paragraph that wishes them well.

## The ending

End on an image or an instruction, not a summary.

> And I'd bet money all three of you still check this chat first when
> something good happens.

> Somebody call first.

> One of you should book the table.

Then stop. No sign-off.
