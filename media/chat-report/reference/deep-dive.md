# The deep dive

After the main report, offer one. The user names a question and you go all the
way down on it, using the same chat and the same voice.

Offer it like this — three or four options drawn from what you actually found,
not generic ones:

> If you want me to go further on one thing: **what he isn't telling you about
> the new job**, **whether this survives the summer**, or **the three weeks last
> spring nobody in this chat has mentioned since**. Or ask your own.

## How a deep dive differs from the report

- **One question, answered completely.** No cast sections, no survey. If the
  question is "does he actually want this", every word serves that.
- **More evidence, less summary.** The main report samples the proof. This one
  shows it — long exchanges, exact dates, counts across time.
- **It's allowed to conclude.** The main report diagnoses. A deep dive answers
  the question asked, in the first paragraph, and spends the rest earning it.
- **Corrections go first.** If reading closely changed your mind about anything
  in the main report, open with that. Two or three lines. It buys you
  everything else.

## Shape

1. **The answer**, in under 40 words, before any working.
2. **What the chat actually shows** — the evidence, in chronological order,
   dated. This is most of it.
3. **What the evidence doesn't cover** — the honest limits, named specifically.
4. **What follows from it.**

Same length rules: 1,500–3,000 words, nothing over 400 words per section.

## Common asks, and how to handle each

**"What are they not telling me?"** — Look for subjects that get one-word
answers, questions returned as questions, plans that never firm up, and the
gaps between how they talk to you and how they talk about others. Say what the
pattern is, and separate it from what the pattern means, which you usually
cannot know.

**"Can this be fixed?"** — Answer yes or no first. Then the conditions, and the
one thing that has to happen before anything else. Then say honestly how likely
each person is to do it, based on whether they've ever done it before in this
chat.

**"Who was in the wrong?"** — Split method from substance. You can usually
judge how someone handled it with total confidence and what they were handling
with none. Say which is which.

**"What should I say to them?"** — Write the actual message. In their voice, at
their length, using their words. Then a short note on what each part is doing,
what to cut if it's too much, and when to send it. Give the honest version, not
the diplomatic one — and say clearly which parts they'll be tempted to delete
and shouldn't.

**"Do they like me / love me / want me?"** — This one gets the strictest
evidence rule in the skill. Reaching, replying and initiating are facts. What
they mean is a read, and you say it's a read. Never manufacture certainty here;
it's the question people act on.

**"Read the last six months again"** — A time-bounded rerun of the main report,
which is legitimate and often the most useful one. Compare directly with the
earlier period and quantify the drift.
