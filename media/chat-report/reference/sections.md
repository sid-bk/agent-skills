# The report: section by section

Use these sections, in this order. Skip one only if the chat genuinely has
nothing to put in it — an empty section is worse than a missing one. Add a
section of your own if the chat demands it; a chat with a business in it, a
custody arrangement, an illness, or a dead friend earns its own heading.

Each section gets a plain heading in the report's own words. Don't reuse the
generic titles below verbatim — turn them into the specific thing this chat is
doing. "The roles each of you plays" becomes "One of you is the planner, the
other two are the audience." Keep an emoji at the front of each heading; one, and it
should be doing work.

---

## Opening (no heading)

Three to six sentences. Lead with the scale in real terms, then the single
observation that reframes the whole thing.

- The count, the span, and two or three concrete things the chat is made of:
  "Two years. 4,210 messages. Two cities. One trip that never happened."
- Then the reframe. Not a summary — a claim. "This was not a book club. This
  was a support line with a reading list attached."
- If the chat has stopped, land the stop hard and date it.

Never open with what you're about to do. Open in the middle.

---

## 1. What this actually is

The chat's real function, as opposed to its stated one. A group named for jokes
that operates as an emergency service. Two friends who are actually running a
small business. A couple whose chat is a scheduling app with feelings attached.

Prove it with infrastructure, not vibes: shared accounts, standing rituals,
recurring logistics, the things they do for each other without being asked, the
words they invented. Name the chat's own name and what it reveals.

End on one line that fixes the frame for the rest of the report.

---

## 2. The cast

One section per person. This is the longest part of the report and the reason
people read it. In a one-on-one, both people get one. In a group, everyone with
a real presence gets one; someone with 40 messages in three years gets a
paragraph inside another section, and the fact that they're barely there is the
observation.

Each person's section:

- **Heading:** their name, then what they are in six words or fewer. "Alex: the
  one who narrates so nobody asks." "Sam: the one who leaves without
  saying."
- **Open with their number** and turn it immediately into meaning. "You sent 46%
  of the messages. Nearly three thousand. And you are the person the other two
  need least."
- **Their moves** — the three or four things they do over and over, each with an
  example. Not adjectives. Moves.
- **Their tell** — the small repeated thing they don't know they're doing. The
  joke they make every time a certain subject comes up. The word they use only
  when they're upset. The way they end conversations.
- **What they're afraid of**, sourced. Point at the moments where the fear leaks
  through.
- **The cost** — what this way of being has bought them and what it's costing
  them, in this chat, with these people.
- **The unflattering line.** Every person gets one sentence that would sting to
  read. Land it and move on. Don't apologise for it and don't soften it with a
  compliment in the same breath.

Address them directly: "Alex, you..." Second person throughout the cast
sections.

Be even-handed in weight, not in verdict. Everyone gets the same word count.
Nobody is graded on a curve.

---

## 3. Who's actually running this

Power, and how it's distributed. Almost always split between different people:
one holds the airtime, one holds the moral authority, one holds the final word,
one holds the exit. Name who holds what, and how you know.

Then the imbalance nobody has said out loud. The person who talks most is
frequently the person with the least power, and that sentence, when it's true,
is the most useful one in the report.

---

## 4. The loop

The sequence this chat runs on repeat. Write it as an actual chain:

> A gives too much → B and C feel crowded → they pull back gently → A reads it
> as abandonment → A gives more → repeat.

Then prove it happened by dating three separate rounds of it. Then say roughly
how many times it has run. Then say what it costs each time, and what it would
take to break — in one sentence, not a plan.

Big chats often have two loops. Two is fine. Three means you haven't found the
real one yet.

---

## 5. The thing everyone can see and nobody has typed

One per person, ideally. The true thing sitting in plain sight that has never
once appeared in the chat as a sentence. It's usually visible in what gets
joked about most, what gets changed the subject on, and what one person says at
2am and nobody picks up.

Short section. One line each, sourced. Don't explain it to death.

---

## 6. What's unsaid, and why

Different from section 5: these are the live conflicts and feelings that both
sides know about and have agreed not to open. Number them, three to five.

For each: what it is, the evidence it exists, who is protecting whom by not
saying it, and what it's costing to keep the lid on.

Include the ambiguous one. If somebody's feelings for somebody else are
unresolved in the text, say that it's unresolved and show the exchanges that
keep it that way — don't resolve it for them.

---

## 7. Who's reaching harder

Who reaches first, who replies fastest, who lets things sit, who double-texts,
who never breaks a silence. Numbers here, plainly said.

Then the meaning, carefully. Reaching more is not loving more, and the report
should say so if the evidence says so — sometimes the fastest replier is the
most anxious person, not the most devoted one. But if one person is genuinely
carrying the relationship, say it in one sentence and don't hedge.

In a group: who each person answers fastest, which pairs are actually a chat
inside the chat, and who is being left on read by whom.

---

## 8. What they're getting right

Real, and specific, and not a consolation prize placed after the criticism to
be nice. Most chats contain something most people never build. Name it exactly.

The permissions they've granted each other. The subjects that got normalised
here that are impossible almost everywhere else. The practical love — the money
sent, the flights checked, the person who stayed on the phone. The best single
moment in the whole chat, quoted.

---

## 9. What's missing

The things you'd expect in a bond this close, and didn't find. Curiosity that
only runs one way. Nobody ever asking a follow-up question. No apology in
three years. No plan ever surviving contact with a calendar. Silence around
money, or family, or the future.

Say what the absence is doing to them.

---

## 10. The moment it turned

If the chat has a break, a fade, or a before-and-after: find it, date it, quote
it, and say what actually happened as against what each person thinks happened.

Be exact about method versus substance — "I don't know whether what she said
was fair. I know you decided it wasn't within the hour, without asking her a
single question."

If the chat is still alive and healthy, replace this with **the pressure
point**: the thing most likely to break it, and the conditions under which it
does.

---

## 11. Where this is heading

A prediction, in the open, with a timeframe. Twelve months out. Say what
happens if nothing changes, and be specific enough to be wrong.

Then, briefly, the best version that's actually reachable from here — not the
ideal one. Two or three sentences, and the one concrete thing that would have
to happen first.

---

## 12. What this gave each of you

One short block per person. What they actually got out of this, stated without
irony and without taking it back. This is where you're allowed to be generous,
because by now you've earned it.

Then the closing line: the image or the instruction the whole report has been
walking toward. One sentence. Land it and stop.

---

# Conditional sections

Use when earned. Keep them short — they're the texture, not the substance.

- **The dictionary.** Words and phrases this chat invented, with real
  definitions and the date of first use. Only if there are at least four.
- **The awards.** Six to ten one-liners: most likely to leave on read, best
  message of the year (quoted), the worst take, the apology that wasn't one.
  Funny is allowed. Ranking is required.
- **The receipts.** The five best quotes in the chat, unaccompanied.
- **The one-sided pairs.** In groups of four or more: who talks to whom, and
  which pairs never speak.
- **The timeline.** Eras with names and date ranges, if the volume chart shows
  clear phases. Three to five eras, one line each.
- **The thing that got skated past.** If a real crisis appeared and the room
  moved on in under a minute — see the safety rules in SKILL.md.

# One-on-one vs group

**One-on-one:** cut section 3 down to a paragraph inside section 7. Go deeper
on sections 5, 6 and 7. "Who's reaching harder" carries much more weight and
deserves the most numbers in the report.

**Group of 3:** the triangle is the story. Every group of three has two people
who are closer and one who knows it. Say who, and say what that person does
about it.

**Group of 4+:** the sub-groups are the story. Which pairs are the real bonds,
who is the glue holding unrelated people together, who would still be in a
group chat with whom if the glue left.

**Family chats:** watch for the parent-child dynamic surviving into adulthood,
the one child who does the logistics, and what everyone performs versus what
they say in the one-on-ones you can see referenced.

**Dead chats:** the last message is the most important message in the file.
Quote it, say who sent it, and say what it was doing.

# Where each section lands on the page

The report is built as four tabs (see `html.md`). Sections map like this:

- **The Read** — the opening, 1, 3, 4, 5, 6, 9, 10, 11, 12.
- **The Cast** — 2, one `.person` block each.
- **The Numbers** — 7, plus the charts, each with its paragraph of reading.
- **Receipts** — the dictionary, the awards, the best quotes, the eras.

Section 8 (what they're getting right) goes wherever it lands best — usually
The Read, just before what's missing.
